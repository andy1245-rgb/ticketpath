# Canada — ticket resale price law, federal and provincial

Research record for the pricing engine's jurisdiction rules table. Descriptive only, no advice.
Compiled 2026-11-08. Every claim below is tied to a URL that was fetched; where a page could not be
read, the gap is written down instead of filled.

**Headline for the engine, 2026-11-08:** price restriction exists in **two** jurisdictions, not four.
Ontario added a cap in April 2026 (it had none when the Ticket Sales Act was passed in 2017) and
Quebec's dates from a 2011 amendment to its Consumer Protection Act. British Columbia's Act is in force and expressly does not regulate
price. Everywhere else in scope here, no ticket-resale price instrument was located.

## Canada (federal)

1. **Law on resale pricing?** No. There is no federal ticket-resale price instrument. What exists is
   general consumer-protection law that bites on how a ticket price is *displayed*, not on how high
   it may go.
2. **Instrument.** Competition Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. C-34, s. 52 (false or misleading representation)
   and s. 52.01, including the drip-pricing provision at s. 52.01(4.1). Read at
   <https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/C-34/section-52.01.html>; the page states the Act is
   "current to 2026-10-19 and last amended on 2026-03-26".
3. **Cap basis.** None. s. 52.01(4.1) makes a price representation unlawful where the advertised
   price is not attainable because of fixed obligatory charges or fees, other than a charge imposed
   by statute. That is a total-price display rule; a seller may still price a ticket at any level.
4. **Coverage.** Any person promoting a business interest or the supply of a product — so both
   primary sellers and resale platforms, in every province and territory. Not ticket-specific.
5. **Penalty and enforcement.** Offence under s. 52.01(6): on indictment, a fine in the court's
   discretion or up to 14 years, or both; on summary conviction, a fine of up to $200,000 or up to
   one year, or both. Enforced by the Competition Bureau and the Director of Public Prosecutions;
   s. 52.01 also sits alongside the reviewable-conduct track in Part VII.1.
6. **Dates.** In force; Act current to 2026-10-19, last amended 2026-03-26.
7. **Unknowns.** The page as fetched is one section; whether any federal bill specifically about
   ticket bots or resale pricing is before Parliament in 2026 was not established (no reliable
   search index was reachable — see Method notes).

## Ontario

1. **Law on resale pricing? Yes — and this changed in 2026.** The Ticket Sales Act, 2017 as passed
   contained no price cap: it was refunds, disclosure, a bot offence and a possession rule. A cap was
   inserted by the 2026 budget omnibus and is in force.
2. **Instruments.**
   - Ticket Sales Act, 2017, S.O. 2017, c. 33, Sched. 3 — <https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/17t33>
     (e-Laws is a JavaScript application; the text was rendered through a reader proxy, see Method
     notes).
   - Plan to Protect Ontario Act (Budget Measures), 2026, S.O. 2026, c. 2 (Bill 97), Schedule 16 —
     <https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/S26002>, assented to April 24, 2026. Schedule 16 s. 9(1)
     brings the Schedule into force on Royal Assent, except ss. 1–2 (July 1, 2026).
3. **Cap basis.** s. 2(3): a ticket may not be offered or facilitated on the secondary market "for an
   amount that exceeds the total price paid when the ticket was purchased from the primary seller,
   plus any applicable fees, service charges and taxes charged by the secondary seller or operator of
   a secondary ticketing platform." So the ceiling is the buyer's original all-in price plus the
   resale side's own fees — not a percentage over face, and not a fixed dollar figure. "Total price"
   is defined in s. 1 as base price plus applicable fees, service charges and taxes. s. 2(3.1) stops
   a secondary seller charging its own fee on top when the platform already charges one, unless the
   regulations permit it. s. 1.2 imputes a total price for tickets obtained for no money. s. 2(4)
   exempts sales for the benefit of a registered charity.
4. **Coverage.** All tickets to recreational, sporting or cultural events, or other prescribed events,
   in Ontario (s. 1 definition of "ticket") — no designated-events list. Binds primary sellers,
   secondary sellers and secondary ticketing platform operators ("ticket business", s. 1). Also
   requires, on a resale, disclosure of the total price when bought from the primary seller and the
   total price charged on resale, plus proof of the original price before a platform may facilitate a
   sale (Schedule 16 ss. 4–5).
5. **Penalty and enforcement.** Contravening s. 2 is an offence (s. 35(1)(b)(i)). Fine on conviction:
   up to $50,000 or up to two years less a day imprisonment, or both, for an individual; up to
   $250,000 for a corporation (s. 35(5)). Separately, the Director may impose an administrative
   penalty of up to $25,000 per contravention (s. 30(3), as amended 2019, c. 7, Sched. 58, s. 9).
   Enforcement is by the Director designated under the Ministry of Consumer and Business Services
   Act, with appeals to the Licence Appeal Tribunal (ss. 30–34). The offence provisions reach
   corporations' officers and directors who fail to take reasonable care (s. 35(3)).
6. **Dates.** The Act is in force and appears as a current consolidation with dated amendments; the
   original 2017 commencement provision is omitted from the consolidation, so the day the 2017 duties
   began is not stated here. s. 2(1) as amended by 2019, c. 7, Sched. 58 is in force from 2021-01-01;
   the price cap and fee rule in force 2026-04-24;
   ss. 1–2 of Schedule 16 (definitions and the secondary-market definition) in force 2026-07-01.
   Some 2019 disclosure amendments to s. 5 remain "not in force" (proclamation).
7. **Unknowns.** The 2026 regulation prescribing administrative-penalty amounts and any fee-cap detail
   under the new s. 2(3.1) was not fetched, so the operative penalty figure and any prescribed fee
   limit are unconfirmed. The commencement of the not-in-force s. 5 disclosure rewrite is unknown.

## Quebec

1. **Law on resale pricing? Yes — a genuine cap, in force since 2012.** Quebec is the oldest of the
   two caps, and its form is a ceiling at the producer-authorised vendor's announced price.
2. **Instrument.** Consumer Protection Act, CQLR c. P-40.1, ss. 236.0.1 and 236.1–236.6, read at
   <https://www.legisquebec.gouv.qc.ca/en/document/cs/P-40.1>. History on the face of the sections:
   s. 236.1 originated with 2011, c. 22 (Bill 25, in force June 2012), was amended by 2018, c. 14 and
   again by 2026, c. 16.
3. **Cap basis.** s. 236.1, second paragraph: "The resale price charged for a ticket may not be higher
   than the price announced by the vendor authorized by the producer of the event, unless (a) the
   consent of the producer … to the resale of the ticket at a higher price was obtained in writing
   first; (b) the ticket is resold in compliance with the agreement entered into with the producer …
   and (c) the maximum resale price of the ticket agreed to by the producer … has been expressly
   brought to the attention of the consumer before the resale." So: cap at the announced primary
   price, with a written producer-consent exception that has to be disclosed. No percentage and no
   dollar figure. s. 236.5 requires an itemised breakdown showing the value fixed when the ticket was
   issued and the nature of every fee.
4. **Coverage.** Sales and resales to consumers by merchants, i.e. businesses in the business of
   selling tickets, including resale platforms; s. 2.2 extends the ticket sections to merchant-to-
   merchant resale contracts. Not limited to designated events. Related duties: platform must state it
   is a resale platform and that cheaper tickets may exist from the authorised vendor (s. 236.0.1);
   bot ban and ban on reselling bot-obtained tickets (s. 236.2); cancellation/modification notice
   (ss. 236.2.1, 236.2.2); refund information before resale (s. 236.3); possession or control
   (s. 236.4); platform that enables a third party to resell is deemed to be reselling (s. 236.6).
5. **Penalty and enforcement.** s. 278 covers contraventions of ss. 236.0.1 to 236.5: minimum fine
   $2,500 (natural person) / $5,000 (other), maximum $62,500 (natural person) / $125,000 (other) or 5%
   of worldwide turnover for the preceding fiscal year, whichever is greater; fines double for a
   second or subsequent offence (s. 281). Enforcement by the Office de la protection du consommateur
   (president) and by penal prosecution; the court may also order compensation to the consumer.
6. **Dates.** s. 236.1 is in force; its amending statute is 2011, c. 22 (the exact commencement date was
   not read, and a secondary pointer placed it in June 2012); 2018, c. 14 amendments in force; 2026, c. 16
   amendments (ss. 236.0.1, 236.2.1, 236.2.2, 236.5, 236.6) appear in the current consolidation, so in
   force. Current consolidation retrieved 2026-11-08.
7. **Unknowns.** The exact commencement date of each 2018 and 2026 amendment was not read (the
   consolidation shows them as in force without the historical date). The 2026, c. 16 amending Act's
   own title was not fetched, so the bill number behind it is not stated here.

## British Columbia

1. **Law on resale pricing? No.** The Ticket Sales Act is in force and regulates disclosure, refunds,
   bots and possession — not price. The regulator says so in terms.
2. **Instruments.**
   - Ticket Sales Act, SBC 2019, c. 13, assented to May 16, 2019, "current to November 3, 2026" —
     <https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/19013>.
   - Consumer Protection BC, "Don't get played: What to know before buying sports tickets in BC",
     posted 2026-10-14 — <https://www.consumerprotectionbc.ca/2026/10/dont-get-played-what-to-know-before-buying-sports-tickets-in-bc/>.
3. **Cap basis.** No price cap. The regulator's words: "The Ticket Sales Act doesn't regulate ticket
   prices, so costs can still vary, especially for popular games and resale tickets." The statute
   requires instead: total price plus itemised fees and taxes, and face value printed on the ticket
   (s. 4); currency, seat location and terms disclosure (s. 5); refund guarantees for secondary sales
   (s. 6); secondary seller identity (s. 7); a bar on related primary/secondary sellers making tickets
   available unless the primary seller first offered them to the public (s. 8); no selling tickets not
   in the seller's possession or control (s. 9); bot-software offence and a duty on primary sellers to
   cancel suspected bot purchases (s. 3).
4. **Coverage.** Ticket service providers — primary sellers, secondary sellers, secondary ticketing
   platform operators — including out-of-province providers who must maintain an accessible BC address
   (s. 10). All events; no designated list. The regulator states it does not reach private sales
   between individuals.
5. **Penalty and enforcement.** Offence under s. 20; fine up to $10,000 or 12 months, or both, for an
   individual, up to $100,000 for a corporation, and the court may increase a fine by up to three times
   the estimated monetary benefit (s. 21); up to $1,000 to the Consumer Advancement Fund and
   compensation to the ticket purchaser may be ordered (ss. 21(4), 22). Administered by the director
   under the Act — Consumer Protection BC — with inspections, administrative penalties (s. 15) and
   court actions by the director (s. 12).
6. **Dates.** Assented 2019-05-16; s. 30 provides that the Act comes into force by regulation of the
   Lieutenant Governor in Council. The Act is presented by the King's Printer as current and the
   regulator applies it in October 2026, so it is in force; the commencement regulation's date was not
   read.
7. **Unknowns.** The commencement date (regulation number and date) is not established from the pages
   fetched. The Tables of Legislative Changes entry, which would give it, was not opened.

## Method notes

- Every instrument text above was read from the official publisher: e-Laws (Ontario), Les
  Publications du Québec / legisquebec, the King's Printer for British Columbia (bclaws), and Justice
  Laws (federal). No secondary source supplied a quote.
- Ontario's e-Laws and Quebec's legisquebec are JavaScript applications that return an empty shell to a
  plain fetch; both were rendered through a text-extraction reader proxy pointed at the official URL.
  The text is the publisher's; the transport was not. The same URL is cited so a reader can check it in
  a browser.
- Search engines were unusable from this host on this date: the free multi-source search returned
  degraded results (Wikipedia and Google News redirects), DuckDuckGo's HTML endpoint answered with a
  challenge page, Bing returned commerce results and ignored quoted site filters, Mojeek required
  JavaScript. URLs were therefore reached by publisher structure (e-Laws' own autocomplete API for the
  Ontario statute alias; the regulator's page for the BC document id) rather than by search.
- CanLII returned a JavaScript wall and was not used.
