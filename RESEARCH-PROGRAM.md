# MASTER RESEARCH PROGRAM
# Predictive Ticket Resale Markets, Primary-Market Price Optimization & Risk-Aware Algorithmic Pricing

## 0. PROGRAM OBJECTIVE

You are the orchestrator of a multi-phase, dynamically allocated research and engineering program.

Your mission is to coordinate an extensive investigation into the mathematics, economics, statistical relationships, market dynamics, predictive algorithms, and optimization techniques necessary to build an efficient ticket-pricing engine.

The ultimate system must:

1. Infer what tickets are realistically worth in the secondary market.
2. Predict how those values will change over time.
3. Estimate the probability of an actual transaction at different prices.
4. Understand how external events, market conditions, supply, demand, and human behavior influence prices.
5. Determine how much secondary-market value can rationally be captured through primary-market pricing.
6. Quantify the risks of higher prices, reduced conversion, and unsold inventory.
7. Optimize pricing decisions using expected economic value rather than simplistic markups.
8. Operate efficiently with incomplete data and limited computational resources.

The final deliverable must be sufficiently rigorous and complete that an engineering agent can implement the system without having to repeat the underlying research.

This is a discovery-driven research program.

The supplied topics, variables, mathematical methods, and candidate algorithms are starting points, not boundaries. Agents must independently discover relevant mechanisms, techniques, and relationships that have not been explicitly mentioned.

Do not prematurely commit to a particular model architecture.

Do not assume the best solution is the most sophisticated model.

Do not confuse theoretical elegance with predictive usefulness.

The objective is to identify the smallest practical architecture that captures the greatest amount of economically useful information.

# 1. RESEARCH DIRECTOR RESPONSIBILITIES

Follow the existing orchestration and agent-handoff protocol.

In addition, operate according to the following research-management principles.

### 1.1 Dynamic allocation

Agent assignments are temporary research roles, not permanent positions.

At the beginning of each phase, determine:
- What is already established.
- What remains unanswered.
- Which uncertainties are most important.
- Which questions are independent.
- Which questions depend on other findings.
- Which investigations are likely to produce the greatest value.
- How many agents are justified for each investigation.

Allocate agents accordingly.

Reallocate agents whenever new findings materially change the research priorities.

An agent that has completed its original assignment should not automatically continue researching the same topic. Its next assignment should be selected based on the current research state.

An agent may investigate a new domain in a subsequent phase if that is the highest-value use of available resources.

### 1.2 Research priority

Prioritize investigations according to:

1. Potential impact on final predictive accuracy.
2. Potential impact on pricing profitability.
3. Importance to the validity of the overall mathematical framework.
4. Current uncertainty or lack of evidence.
5. Availability of actionable data.
6. Likelihood of producing useful discoveries.
7. Implementation feasibility.

Do not allocate agents based solely on how interesting a subject appears.

A mathematically interesting discovery that cannot realistically be measured or implemented may have less value than a modest improvement in transaction-price estimation.

### 1.3 Dependency-aware research

Identify dependencies between investigations.

For example:
- Demand estimation may depend on transaction-price reconstruction.
- Primary pricing optimization may depend on demand elasticity.
- Risk optimization may depend on probabilistic price forecasting.
- External-variable selection may depend on the baseline forecasting architecture.
- Implementation may depend on identifying accessible data sources.

Do not dispatch downstream investigations with insufficient upstream information.

However, do not unnecessarily serialize independent investigations. Parallelize wherever useful.

### 1.4 Continuous discovery

Never treat the original research checklist as the complete research space.

After each phase, identify new research questions arising from the findings.

Examples:
- A discovered relationship may suggest a new causal mechanism.
- A new statistical method may suggest a different market representation.
- A data limitation may require an alternative inference technique.
- A market anomaly may reveal a previously unconsidered external variable.
- A modeling failure may suggest a different mathematical formulation.

Convert meaningful discoveries into explicit follow-up assignments.

Continue expanding the investigation when expected research value justifies the cost.

# 3. PHASED RESEARCH EXECUTION

The program consists of eight phases.

Phases are logical research stages, not fixed time periods or fixed agent counts.

The orchestrator decides when to transition based on research maturity.

A phase can be revisited if subsequent findings reveal important gaps.

## PHASE 1 — FOUNDATIONAL DISCOVERY AND PROBLEM DECOMPOSITION

### Objective

Establish the complete research landscape before committing to specific mathematical solutions.

This phase is primarily exploratory.

Agents should discover what the problem actually consists of, including overlooked market mechanisms, mathematical approaches, external influences, data requirements, and dependencies.

### Research domains

Investigate:

**1. Market structure**
- Primary ticket markets.
- Secondary ticket markets.
- Market fragmentation.
- Buyers and sellers.
- Reseller behavior.
- Platform economics.
- Listing and transaction mechanics.
- Market liquidity.
- Price discovery.

**2. Economic structure**
- Supply and demand.
- Willingness to pay.
- Price elasticity.
- Market equilibrium.
- Consumer behavior.
- Strategic seller behavior.
- Inventory expiration.
- Revenue management.

**3. Statistical structure**
- Time-dependent observations.
- Incomplete observations.
- Censored transactions.
- Selection bias.
- Sparse histories.
- Market heterogeneity.
- Nonstationarity.
- Regime changes.

**4. Mathematical opportunity discovery**
Explore methods from:
- Econometrics.
- Quantitative finance.
- Operations research.
- Statistical mechanics.
- Information theory.
- Survival analysis.
- Stochastic processes.
- Bayesian statistics.
- Causal inference.
- Optimization.

**5. External influence discovery**

Independently discover variables that could influence ticket values.

Do not restrict discovery to conventional calendar, weather, event, demand, supply, and economic variables.

### Required outcome

## PHASE 3 — CROSS-DOMAIN INTERACTION AND CAUSAL DISCOVERY

### Objective

Investigate relationships that cannot be understood by studying individual variables or models in isolation.

This phase is essential.

Ticket prices are determined by interacting systems, not independent feature contributions.

Reallocate agents to investigate connections between findings from different domains.

### Required investigation

Investigate how combinations of factors affect prices.

Examples:

- Inventory depletion conditional on demand intensity.
- Time-to-event conditional on event popularity.
- Resale premiums conditional on primary inventory availability.
- Price momentum conditional on seller competition.
- News impact conditional on announcement timing.
- Weather conditional on transportation accessibility.
- Social attention conditional on historical demand.
- Consumer spending conditions conditional on event category.
- Market liquidity conditional on ticket quality.
- Price elasticity conditional on remaining inventory.
- Additional primary inventory conditional on resale market scarcity.

These are examples, not an exhaustive list.

Discover further interactions independently.

### Investigate feedback loops

Consider that:

- Primary prices influence resale supply.
- Resale prices influence primary willingness to pay.
- Primary inventory releases alter secondary-market pricing.
- Seller price reductions influence competing sellers.
- Market visibility influences buyer expectations.
- Demand changes influence seller listing decisions.
- Pricing policies alter the data subsequently observed.

Investigate whether the market should be modeled as a dynamic feedback system.

### Causal investigation

For important relationships, determine whether the observed association is causal, predictive, or both.

Investigate possible confounding, reverse causality, simultaneity, and selection effects.

### Required outcome

Produce:
- Interaction registry.
- Causal mechanism diagrams.
- Important conditional relationships.
- Proposed interaction features.
- Dynamic market feedback models.
- Evidence supporting or contradicting each relationship.
- Prioritized experiments.

### Phase transition criteria

Move forward when the most economically important interactions are understood well enough to inform feature engineering and model architecture.

## PHASE 5 — PROBABILISTIC PRICING AND ECONOMIC OPTIMIZATION

### Objective

Translate the predictive models into economically optimal pricing decisions.

This is where forecasting becomes a pricing system.

### Research requirements

Investigate how to combine:

- Predicted resale-price distributions.
- Expected net resale proceeds.
- Demand curves.
- Price elasticity.
- Sale probabilities.
- Available inventory.
- Time remaining.
- Unsold inventory costs.
- Price adjustment opportunities.
- Risk tolerance.
- Operational constraints.

Develop and compare static and dynamic pricing strategies.

Investigate the effect of increasing primary prices by different percentages of the predicted resale premium.

Do not assume any fixed premium-capture percentage is optimal.

Derive the optimal price from expected economic value.

### Required mathematical outcomes

Develop:

1. An expected-revenue function.
2. An expected-profit function.
3. A sell-through probability model.
4. An inventory expiration model.
5. A risk-adjusted objective.
6. A dynamic pricing policy, if justified.
7. Appropriate constraints and price bounds.

Investigate whether pricing should be optimized independently for each ticket or jointly across inventory.

Investigate whether the optimization should consider cannibalization and correlated ticket demand.

### Required outcome

Produce:
- Complete mathematical optimization formulation.
- Derivations.
- Assumptions.
- Risk treatment.
- Sensitivity analysis.
- Numerical examples.
- Recommended decision policy.

### Phase transition criteria

Move forward when the proposed pricing strategy is mathematically coherent and its required predictive inputs are defined.

# 4. GLOBAL RESEARCH STANDARDS

These standards apply throughout every phase.

## 4.1 Unbounded investigation

The initial research topics are not exhaustive.

Agents should investigate anything with a plausible connection to ticket market behavior.

This includes techniques and findings from fields that do not ordinarily study tickets.

For example, a technique developed for financial markets, airline revenue management, online advertising auctions, used-vehicle pricing, labor markets, inventory management, or exchange-traded assets may offer a useful solution.

Explore cross-domain transfer deliberately.

## 4.2 Mechanism before feature inclusion

Do not add variables simply because they are available.

Establish the mechanism through which a variable could influence:
- Buyers.
- Sellers.
- Supply.
- Demand.
- Liquidity.
- Willingness to pay.
- Transaction probability.
- Price expectations.

Then evaluate whether it offers incremental predictive value.

## 4.3 Evidence classification

Classify every important conclusion as:

A. Directly supported by ticket-market empirical evidence.

B. Supported by empirical research in a related market.

C. Supported by mathematical theory or established statistical methodology.

D. Plausible but untested hypothesis.

E. Speculative research opportunity.

Never present a hypothesis as an established finding.

## 4.4 Mathematical rigor

Explain what the mathematics actually does.

Where relevant, provide:
- Equations.
- Assumptions.
- Derivations.
- Estimation methods.
- Parameter interpretations.
- Computational complexity.
- Limitations.

Do not merely list model names.

## 4.5 Source quality

Prioritize:
1. Original academic publications.
2. Peer-reviewed empirical research.
3. Working papers from credible researchers.
4. Official technical documentation.
5. Verified industry datasets and reports.
6. Reproducible technical research.

Follow important citations to original sources.

Provide working links and bibliographic details.

## 4.6 No invented evidence

Never fabricate datasets, accuracy improvements, experiments, statistical significance, historical observations, or source capabilities.

If evidence does not exist, say so.

## 4.7 Predictive versus causal usefulness

A variable can be useful for forecasting without having a causal effect.

A causal variable can have little incremental forecasting value if its information is already reflected in market prices.

Distinguish these concepts consistently.

## 4.8 Temporal correctness

All models must respect what was knowable at prediction time.

Investigate look-ahead bias, target leakage, delayed information availability, revised historical data, and retrospective feature contamination.

## 4.9 Practicality

The objective is not to build a giant model.

The objective is to extract as much useful predictive and economic information as possible while maintaining low computational cost and operational simplicity.

# 5. RESEARCH COMPLETION POLICY

The program should not terminate merely because all eight phases have been visited.

Completion should be determined by research maturity.

Before concluding, the orchestrator must establish that:

- The fundamental market mechanisms have been investigated.
- Important overlooked variables and interactions have been explored.
- Transaction-price inference has a defensible solution.
- Future price prediction has a defined architecture.
- Sale probability has a defined methodology.
- Uncertainty is incorporated into pricing decisions.
- Primary-price optimization has a coherent economic objective.
- Data requirements are realistic.
- Validation is rigorous.
- Implementation is sufficiently specified.
- Remaining uncertainties are explicitly documented.

Use diminishing marginal research value as the stopping principle.

If a newly discovered question could materially change the architecture or economic outcome, investigate it before declaring the program complete.

If further research is unlikely to alter the implementation or improve expected performance meaningfully, record the remaining uncertainty and conclude.

**The ultimate measure of success is not research volume. It is whether the investigation produces a defensible, empirically testable, economically rational, computationally efficient ticket-pricing engine.**
